Same battery, same state,
two very different bills

A factory pays for electricity twice. Once for the power itself, and once to the network company for delivering it. That second bill is usually set by the single biggest burst of power the factory ever draws.

That is a problem for a thermal battery, because drawing in big bursts is exactly how it buys cheap electricity in the first place.

But networks do not all measure that burst the same way. Some only look during set hours, say 5pm to 9pm in summer, so a battery can simply not charge then. Others look at every hour of the year, and there is nowhere to hide. That one difference decides whether flexibility is worth anything.

This is the whole argument in one gesture. Under a charge that only looks during set hours, the battery sits out four hours an evening and keeps every bit of its advantage. Under a charge that looks all the time, every gulp sets the number it gets billed on, so it stops gulping and slowly turns back into the ordinary factory it was supposed to beat.

A battery can dodge a window. It cannot dodge "whenever."

What this does and does not do

It models the two structural choices the study is about: how much of your bill rides on peak demand, and whether that peak is time-aware. It is a screening tool, not a billing engine. Real schedules carry bands, ratchets, riders and seasonal detail this flattens, and Spain's six-band structure in particular cannot be expressed in two dials.

It assumes one factory: 10 MW of heat continuously, a 12-hour store, 1%/day standby loss, each market's real forecast horizon. It prices arbitrage only, with no firmness, no reliability value, and no capital cost of any kind, so it is not a payback calculation.

Two reasons its numbers can differ from the tables above. This screener optimises the charge rate across 1× to 8×, while the study fixes it at 4×, so it will often find a gentler, better-paying strategy that the study does not report. And for MISO the study's inflexible buyer takes utility-supplied energy, which is priced differently from the wholesale market both buyers use here.

The published figures in the sections above come from the full tariffs in tariff.py, not from this. The build refuses to ship if the statistics behind this screener cannot reproduce those tariffs exactly.
