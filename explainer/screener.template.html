Same battery, same state,
two very different bills

A factory pays for electricity twice. Once for the power itself, and once to the network company for delivering it. That second bill is usually set by the single biggest burst of power the factory ever draws.

That is a problem for a thermal battery, because drawing in big bursts is exactly how it buys cheap electricity in the first place.

But networks do not all measure that burst the same way. Some only look during set hours, say 5pm to 9pm in summer, so a battery can simply not charge then. Others look at every hour of the year, and a battery has no way around it. That one difference decides whether a battery saves the factory anything at all.

Under a charge that only looks during set hours, the battery sits out four hours an evening and keeps every bit of its advantage. Under a charge that looks all the time, every gulp sets the number it gets billed on. So it stops gulping, and most of what it was saving goes with it.

A battery can dodge a window. It cannot dodge "whenever."

Minnesota is the row that settles the cause, because the same utility offers two tariffs on the same grid, to the same factory, in the same years.

Otter Tail Power's standard tariff bills whatever the meter recorded as your highest moment that month. A battery loses money on it in every one of the eight years, and in all 36 combinations when nine MISO pricing points are checked across four of them. Its Thermal Market Energy Pricing tariff bills a figure agreed in advance instead of the meter reading, and the same battery wins in all eight years.

Nothing changed but one sentence in the rate schedule. That tariff is already filed and approved.

What you are setting. A network bill has two parts. One is charged on every unit of energy you take, and a battery cannot avoid it. What this does and does not do

It models the two structural choices the study is about: how much of your bill rides on peak demand, and whether that peak is time-aware. Treat it as a first screen rather than a bill. Real schedules carry bands, ratchets, riders and seasonal detail this flattens, and Spain's six-band structure in particular cannot be expressed in two dials.

It assumes one factory: 10 MW of heat continuously, a thermal battery holding 12 hours, 1%/day standby loss, each market's real forecast horizon. It prices arbitrage only, with no firmness, no reliability value and no capital cost of any kind, so nothing here is a payback figure.

Two reasons its numbers can differ from the tables above. This screener optimises the charge rate across 1× to 8×, while the study fixes it at 4×, so it will often find a gentler, better-paying strategy that the study does not report. And for MISO the study's inflexible buyer takes utility-supplied energy, which is priced differently from the wholesale market both buyers use here.

The published figures in the sections above are computed from the full tariffs in tariff.py. The build refuses to ship if the statistics behind this screener cannot reproduce those tariffs exactly.
