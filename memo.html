Memorandum

What decides whether a flexible industrial load saves money

Prepared by Hari Dharshini Koundinya Swaminathen September 2026 Spain, South Australia, Minnesota 25 years of published prices

A factory making heat from electricity pays two bills. One for the power. One to the network for delivering it, and that second bill is usually set by the single biggest burst of power the factory ever draws.

A thermal battery wins the first bill by buying in short hard bursts, which is exactly what the second bill punishes. Across three markets and 25 years of published prices, it was always the second bill that decided whether the battery saved money or lost it.

And what decided that bill was which hours the network watches when it looks for your biggest burst. Some watch only certain hours, so a battery puts its bursts elsewhere. Others watch every hour of the year, and it cannot.

South Australia proves it on its own, because two network companies there bill the same factory two different ways. The one watching only summer evenings beat the one watching always by 19 to 44 percentage points, in every one of nine years. Nothing else differs between them.

Minnesota confirms it, and already contains the fix. On Otter Tail Power's standard tariff the battery loses money in all 36 combinations of pricing point and year tested. Under its Thermal Market Energy Pricing tariff, which bills demand on a figure agreed in advance instead of the metered peak, the same battery wins in all eight years. That tariff is filed and approved.

Where this is weakest. Three assumptions could move these numbers, all measured rather than hedged. Australian price visibility is assumed at six hours; at twenty-four the saving runs 49.9% to 73.1%, so 23 points rest on it. TMEP's agreed baseline is not written in the tariff: 10 MW gives +20.6%, 20 MW gives −6.0%. And South Australia is the best of five Australian regions, 22.6% in Tasmania against 49.9% there, so read it as the top of a range.

What I would ask you. What it costs a customer when heat stops, the missing term in every firmness number here. What baseline demand is realistically achievable in a TMEP negotiation. And whether the right benchmark is a gas boiler or an inflexible electric one.

Page 3. Why some tariffs reward a flexible load and others punish it

Three different designs show up, and they are worth telling apart because the third one is not a weaker version of the first two.

South Australia pays you to stay out. Avoiding 5pm to 9pm is worth 8.9% before a single cheap hour is chased. A factory needs no forecasting skill at all to benefit, which makes this the easiest design to build a business on.

Spain does not pay you to stay out, but it stops you being punished. Staying in the cheap bands alone loses 4.9%. Its value is entirely in combination: chasing cheap power creates a big burst, and Spain's six time bands let you put that burst where it is charged almost nothing. That is why doing both is worth 9.7 points more than the two parts added together.

Minnesota punishes both moves. Chasing cheap power alone loses 59.1%, because the burst gets billed. There is no clock to play, so the second move earns almost nothing. Both together still lose.

This is what makes Otter Tail's TMEP tariff the useful case rather than an exception. The same utility offers two tariffs, on the same grid, to the same factory, in the same years. One bills whatever the meter recorded as the highest moment that month. The other bills a figure agreed in advance. Nothing changes but one sentence in the rate schedule, and eight losing years become eight winning ones.

Page 4. Against a gas boiler

A factory choosing today is choosing against a gas boiler, not an electric heater. Gas is cheap, so the comparison turns on what the factory pays for the carbon it emits.

Rather than argue about the right carbon price, the analysis works out the price at which the two come out equal. If that number is below what carbon actually costs, the battery already wins.

In 2018, carbon would have had to cost €101 a tonne before the battery beat gas. By 2025 the figure is below zero, meaning the battery wins even if carbon is free. Europe charges €75.

The two spikes are the European energy crisis, and they are worth understanding rather than removing. Gas more than doubled in 2022, which should have favoured the battery. It did the opposite, because the wholesale electricity the battery buys more than doubled too. Spain's average price ran €168 per MWh that year against €65 in 2025. An energy crisis is not a one-way bet on storage, because both fuels are in it.

The honest qualification is that 2025 is the best year in the series. Ignoring carbon and comparing fuel cost alone, the battery beat gas in only two of the eight Spanish years. The trend is the finding, not the single year.

Page 5. Method, ranges and sources

Everything here models one factory. It needs 10 MW of heat every hour of the year, and it has a thermal battery holding 12 hours of heat, which can be filled at up to four times its average draw. Every figure compares that factory against an identical one with no battery, buying power as it uses it. Network tariffs are held at their 2025 values across every price year, so that only prices vary.

Three things are deliberately left out: what the battery costs to build, what it is worth to have heat available when the grid fails, and any income beyond buying power at better times. So this measures what a battery saves on the electricity bill and nothing else. It does not say whether building one pays for itself.

Each of the three assumptions on page 1 was tested by re-running the whole model across a range of values and recording how far the answer travels. These are measured spreads, not estimates of uncertainty.
